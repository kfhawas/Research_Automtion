Artificial Intelligence

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence

Main idea: AI is the field of computer science focused on building systems that can perform tasks requiring human-like intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, and problem-solving.

Supporting points:

  • Core goals include learning, reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, NLP, perception, and robotics, pursued via search, optimization, logic, and neural networks.
  • Founded as an academic discipline in 1956; went through 'AI winters' of reduced funding before rebounding.
  • Deep learning plus GPUs after 2012, transformers after 2017, and generative AI in the 2020s drove the recent AI boom.
  • Applications span search engines, chatbots, virtual assistants, autonomous vehicles, strategy games, and content generation.
  • Raises ethical concerns around safety, unintended harms, environmental impact, and possible existential risk, prompting calls for regulation.

Key takeaways:

  • AI has moved from a niche academic field to a technology embedded in everyday tools within about 70 years.
  • Its rapid growth is tied to specific technical breakthroughs (GPUs, transformers), not steady linear progress.
  • Capability gains are now outpacing agreement on how to govern the technology safely.

Climate Change

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change

Main idea: Human activity, mainly fossil fuel burning, has driven roughly 1.5°C of global warming since the Industrial Revolution, reshaping Earth's climate system.

Supporting points:

  • Burning coal, oil, and gas, plus deforestation and some farming/industrial practices, releases greenhouse gases that trap heat in the atmosphere.
  • Atmospheric CO2 is now about 50% higher than pre-industrial levels, the highest in millions of years.
  • Effects already visible: more frequent heat waves and wildfires, shrinking glaciers and sea ice, thawing permafrost, and more intense storms and droughts.
  • Long-term consequences such as ocean warming, acidification, and sea-level rise will continue for centuries even if warming is halted soon.
  • Human impacts include flooding, food and water scarcity, disease, economic loss, and migration; the WHO ranks it among the top health threats of the century.

Key takeaways:

  • The warming trend is attributed to human activity, not natural variability alone.
  • Some damage is already locked in regardless of future action.
  • The stakes extend beyond the environment into health, economics, and migration.

Remote Work

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_work

Main idea: Remote work, practiced on a small scale for decades, became mainstream after COVID-19 forced a rapid and lasting shift for white-collar jobs worldwide.

Supporting points:

  • Enabled by decades of technology, from 1970s terminal links to modern cloud collaboration and video conferencing.
  • Proponents cite lower overhead costs, greater employee autonomy and flexibility, less commuting, and access to a wider talent pool.
  • Critics argue it weakens face-to-face collaboration, increases distraction, and can cause isolation.
  • Managing distributed teams isn't new; historical organizations relied on trust, record-keeping, and oversight to manage remote operations.
  • Historical records, such as the 1911 England and Wales census, already tracked whether people worked 'at home,' showing the practice predates modern technology.

Key takeaways:

  • The pandemic accelerated, rather than invented, a long-running trend.
  • The core debate over flexibility and cost savings versus collaboration and connection isn't new either.
  • Effective remote management still comes down to trust and communication systems, not just technology.

Spaced Repetition

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition

Main idea: Spaced repetition is a learning technique that boosts long-term memory by reviewing information at increasing intervals, exploiting the psychological 'spacing effect.'

Supporting points:

  • Commonly used for flashcard-based learning, especially vocabulary acquisition in second-language study.
  • Rooted in Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1880s 'forgetting curve,' which showed memory decays over time unless reinforced.
  • Landauer and Bjork (1978) confirmed spaced review improves recall using face-name association tests.
  • Schacter, Rich, and Stampp (1985) extended the benefit to people with amnesia and other memory disorders.
  • Camp (1989) found the technique also helped Alzheimer's patients retain information longer.

Key takeaways:

  • The technique is backed by over a century of research, not just modern app design.
  • It works across a wide range of learners, from language students to memory-impaired patients.
  • Spacing out review sessions is more effective than cramming for durable memory.
